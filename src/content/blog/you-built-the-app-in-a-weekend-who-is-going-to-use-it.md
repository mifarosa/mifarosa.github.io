# You Built the App in a Weekend. Who Is Going to Use It?

**Demand Validation, Distribution, and the Early Paywall Fallacy in the Vibe Coding Era**

Imagine sitting down at your computer on a Friday evening, telling your AI coding tool what you want, and waking up on Sunday morning with a full-fledged mobile app ready to upload to the App Store. A software development process that once took months, heavy labor, and serious capital now comes down to a single natural-language prompt. Vibe coding, a term popularized by AI researcher Andrej Karpathy in February 2025 and named Collins Dictionary’s Word of the Year for 2025, has removed the barrier to writing code almost entirely, thanks to tools like Cursor, Replit, Lovable, and Bolt.

But while the cost of building software has dropped close to zero, the cost of marketing, acquiring users, and validating market demand has not. If anything, production has become easier while earning visibility and building user trust has become harder than ever. The assumption developers so often fall into, “I needed a tool like this, so everyone must need one,” has filled the app stores with weekend projects: untested, asking for subscriptions before delivering any value, and wandering unprepared into markets already held by established players.

## The Supply Explosion and the False Spring of Digital Shelves

The spread of AI coding agents has created an unprecedented wave of content in mobile app stores. Yet the data shows this wave is not a demand boom but a massive supply boom. According to Sensor Tower, the number of new apps published on the App Store in the first quarter of 2026 rose by a dramatic 84% year over year, reaching 235,800. Across all of 2025, roughly 600 thousand new apps joined the App Store, a 30% annual increase in supply. In the first half of 2026, the number of new apps published approached 560 thousand.

Against this surge in supply, consumer interest and app download habits have stayed almost completely flat. Sensor Tower’s analysis of the same period shows App Store downloads grew by only 3% year over year. In other words, the shelves are filling up fast, while the number of customers walking into the store has barely changed.

The economic consequences of this imbalance are harsh for independent developers. According to RevenueCat’s State of Subscription Apps 2026 report, which examined 115 thousand apps and $16 billion in subscription volume, the share of the pie captured by newly launched apps has shrunk to a critical level.

This data shows that the middle class of the mobile ecosystem is eroding, and that the competitive advantage has moved away from simply being able to write code toward owning a distribution channel and building user trust.

## The “I Need It, So Everyone Does” Fallacy and Startup Post-Mortems

Building a product to solve a personal problem is the starting point of many of the most successful stories in entrepreneurial history. But the biggest trap in this process is jumping straight from step one to “I can sell this to everyone.” A capable developer solving their own need first asks whether they use the product regularly themselves, then how many other people have the same problem, and then how those people solve it today.

According to CB Insights’ 2014 analysis of 101 startup post-mortems, 42% of failed companies cited “no market need” as their main reason. A more recent analysis covering 431 venture-backed startups that shut down between 2023 and 2025 found that, of the 385 companies whose reason for closing could be identified, 43% failed because of weak product-market fit. Although running out of capital (70%) is the final outcome of most shutdowns, the root cause is spending resources on a product with no place in the market.

Funded companies can spend 18 months searching for market fit. The time a solo vibe coder can afford from their own pocket and motivation is usually a few months. That is why starting to write code without validating the market is like putting a roof on a building whose foundation hasn’t been laid.

## The Minefields Unvalidated Vibe Coding Projects Walk Into

The categories weekend-built apps target most often are, paradoxically, the ones with the highest market entry barriers and lock-in effects. Here are some examples I've run into more often than I'd like:

### 1. Classifieds Sites and Second-Hand Marketplaces

Developers often show up with the idea of a more modern classifieds site, implying that the interfaces of platforms like Sahibinden or letgo have aged. Yet Amazon's interface is crowded and far from clean, and it still remains the world's largest marketplace. That's because on marketplaces, the interface doesn't decide the winner. Marketplaces run on a classic chicken-and-egg problem: without buyers, sellers won't post listings, and without sellers, buyers won't come to the platform. Users stay on a platform not because of its design but because the other side is already there. A nicer interface can't overcome that network effect on its own.

In Turkey, for example, Sahibinden.com held an enormous network effect as of April 2025, with 62.8 million monthly active users, 560 million visits, and more than 8 million active listings. The 40.15 million TL administrative fine the Turkish Competition Authority imposed on Sahibinden in 2023, for blocking corporate members from moving their data to other platforms, confirms the scale of the lock-in the market leader has created. On the second-hand fashion side, Dolap, part of Trendyol, put more than 14 million items back into circulation according to its 2025 sustainability data. Expecting users to leave these huge buyer-seller networks just because your interface is prettier is a pipe dream.

### 2. Apartment and Residential Complex Management Software

From the outside, the apartment management sector looks like a simple dues-tracking app, but it carries deep administrative and legal responsibilities. In this space, players like Apsiyon serve 26,103 residential communities, 1.75 million individual units, and 4.28 million users.

More importantly, the purchasing decision for this software is made not by the residents but by professional managers or management companies who carry administrative and legal responsibility. Considering that, as of 2026, administrative fines under Turkey’s KVKK (Personal Data Protection Law) for data security breaches range from 256,357 TL to 17,092,242 TL, a leak from a weekend project’s database creates legal liabilities for the developer that can never be undone.

### 3. Exam Prep and Education Apps

Education is one of the categories where developers ship the most products but where user retention rates are among the lowest. In the Turkish market there are giants like Doping Hafıza, active for 16 years, with a team of 200, more than 100 thousand videos, and over 1 million downloads.

AppsFlyer and Adjust data show that day-30 average retention in education apps stays between 2% and 4%, meaning 96–98% of users abandon the app within 30 days. Putting a subscription wall on the opening screen of an education app that hasn’t yet built a study habit in its users is like trying to fill a leaky bucket with water.

### 4. Super Apps (All-in-One)

The idea of "everything in one app" is among the easiest ideas to code with AI and the hardest to keep alive. With AI coding tools, you can put together a "super app" combining shopping, payments, bookings, and chat in a weekend. But regional tech giants like WeChat or Grab didn't arrive at their current form by offering everything at once. Both first built a daily habit by doing one thing extremely well: one with messaging, the other with ride-hailing. They added the other services over the years, on top of that habit and the trust that came with it.

That's why the real barrier for super apps is not technical but focus. Multiple services offered without a strong wedge can't tell users why they should open the app. Each feature competes with an established rival on its own, and none is strong enough alone to bring users back. The result is the same as the retention picture we saw earlier: users try it once and never open it again. The right order is to become indispensable at one problem first, and to think about expanding only after that habit has taken hold.

## The Early Paywall Fallacy

Asking users for money early is not a mistake in itself. The real mistake is trying to bill users before the value has been proven and the product has been hardened. RevenueCat’s 2026 data shows concretely how paywall choices affect commercial performance:

- **Conversion rates:** At day 35 after install, the median conversion-to-paid rate for apps using a hard paywall is 10.7%. For the freemium model, it stays at 2.1% (roughly a 5x difference).
- **Revenue per user (ARPU):** At day 60 after install, apps with a hard paywall earn an average of $3.09 per user, versus $0.38 for freemium apps.
- **Retention balance:** At the end of the first year, annual subscriber retention is 27% for hard paywall models and 28% for freemium models. So the paywall type doesn’t change users’ long-term loyalty to the product; it only determines when the revenue is collected.
- **Cancellation and refund reality:** 55.4% of cancellations in 3-day trials happen on the very first day (Day 0). Hard paywall apps also have a notably higher refund rate (5.8%) than freemium apps (3.4%).

This quantitative picture points to a strategic reality: a hard paywall works well in tools where the user can grasp the value within the first 60 seconds (for example, a background-removal app or a quick file compression tool). But on platforms where value emerges over time or as the network effect builds (apartment management, classifieds sites, study tracking systems), showing a paywall on the first screen means turning the potential customer away while they are still at the door.

Considering that an average app retains 25% of its users on day 1, 8–13% on day 7, and only 4–6% on day 30, hiding a product that creates no value behind a paywall does nothing but speed up failure.

## The Turkish Local Market and Global Revenue Opportunities

Turkey has a momentum in mobile spending that stands out globally, but its unit price levels are quite low.

- **Market size:** According to Sensor Tower’s State of Mobile 2026 Turkey data, Turkish users spent $1.35 billion on in-app purchases in 2025, putting Turkey among the world’s top 15 markets by this revenue. Spending grew 35% year over year while download numbers stayed flat. At the top of the revenue list are global giants such as TikTok, ChatGPT, YouTube, and Tinder.
- **Price levels:** In the price index of Adapty’s State of In-App Subscriptions 2026 report (US = 1.0), Turkey sits at 0.7, ranking 21st among 22 countries. Weekly subscription prices in Turkey are about 3.5 times cheaper than comparable services in the US.
- **Regional conversion:** In the MEA (Middle East and Africa) region, which includes Turkey, the median trial-to-paid subscription conversion rate is 19.5%, versus 34.2% in North America. Revenue per install at day 14 is $0.10 in MEA and $0.38 in North America.

That is why, according to data reported by Forbes Türkiye, Turkey-based non-gaming mobile app companies earn their roughly $2 billion in annual revenue not from the local market but by exporting directly to the US and European markets (earning dollars and euros). For a vibe coder looking only for TL-based subscriptions in the local market, staying financially afloat is extremely difficult.

## AI-Generated Code and Operational Responsibility: “The Last 20% Needs Human Effort”

The moment you take payment from a user, the relationship between developer and user stops being a hobby project and becomes a legal service agreement. With vibe coding, you can finish the first 80% of a prototype in hours, but the remaining 20%, covering security, data architecture, error handling, and customer support, requires human expertise.

Concrete security and operational failures in the industry prove the point:

- **Code security flaws (Veracode 2025):** In Veracode’s research, which tested more than 100 large language models on 80 different coding tasks, 45% of the generated code contained security vulnerabilities in the OWASP Top 10 categories.
- **Database breaches (CVE-2025-48757):** In a scan of 1,645 apps built on the Lovable platform, 170 apps (10.3%) were found to have forgotten Supabase Row Level Security (RLS) rules, leaving users’ names, emails, and financial records accessible from outside, as reported by Semafor.
- **Live data loss:** In July 2025, in a project by SaaStr founder Jason Lemkin, a Replit AI agent mistakenly deleted a live database covering 1,206 executives and 1,196 companies on command, showing that generative AI can’t be left alone in production systems.

Asking users for credit card details or personal data from an app that an AI wrote over a weekend, without a security audit, shifts the entire risk onto the user.

## A 10-Step Checklist Before You Ask for Money

Coding your idea and launching it in a weekend is a wonderful superpower, but it doesn’t exempt you from market realities. Before publishing your product and putting up a paywall, it’s recommended that you follow this 10-step validation protocol:

1. **Personal use test:** Use the app you built every day yourself for at least 2 to 4 weeks. If even you don’t open it every day, nobody else will open it at all.
2. **Qualitative problem interviews (the Mom Test):** Talk to 10–15 potential users without praising your idea. Instead of “Would you do this?”, ask “When was the last time you ran into this problem, and how much did you spend to solve it?”
3. **Alternatives and switching-cost analysis:** Map how users solve this today with Excel, WhatsApp groups, or an established competitor. Explain in one sentence the legitimate reason they would have to switch to your app.
4. **Niche targeting:** Instead of a broad market like apartment management, pick a narrowed niche you can actually win, such as a dues reminder for buildings with fewer than 10 units.
5. **No-code demand test (smoke test):** Before writing the app, set up a landing page describing your promise, show the price, and collect a waitlist. If no one signs up for the waitlist, no one will download the app either.
6. **Pre-sale validation:** Before launch, try selling a discounted founding-member package to 5–10 people. Real willingness to pay is the strongest demand validation signal.
7. **Micro pilots:** Scale platforms that need a network effect by running them in a single apartment building, a single classroom, or a single neighborhood group.
8. **Measure the aha moment:** Identify the “Aha!” moment where users see value. If the value is clear in the first minute, use an early paywall; if it takes weeks, set up a longer trial or a freemium model.
9. **Technical and security audit:** Fully complete your database Row Level Security (RLS) rules, API key secrecy, environment separation, and KVKK/GDPR privacy notices.
10. **Distribution channel isolation:** Before the product is finished, name exactly where your first 100 users will come from (a specific community, a particular social media channel, direct B2B visits), person by person.

## Conclusion

In the vibe coding era, building an app is just a weekend job. But proving that the app solves a real problem in people’s lives takes patience, data, and the right market strategy. The best moment to ask for money is when the user says “I don’t want to lose this,” not when you say “they’ll definitely use it.”
